Projects at the boundary

You have a project; how should it be managed and evaluated? Where a project sits between the organization and its operations decides what data exists about it. The analysis closes the loop, almost always incompletely.

About 15 minutesReading guide

Why a data course reads about projects

You have a project. Element 3 of your Project Brief names a change, decision, or intervention to evaluate, and in organizations most changes arrive as projects: a new system, a new schedule, a reorganization, a pilot at a few sites. Riverbend has two. SmartShift went live at three sites in 2025. The new HR information system went live the same year.

Managing a project and evaluating it are usually treated as separate jobs. They aren't. How a project is run decides who chose where the change happened, whose time it used up, and who keeps recording things after it ends, and those decisions make the data the evaluation depends on. Read this page as a map of where your data comes from, and where it is likely to break.

A project lives on a boundary

A project begins when a permanent organization authorizes work it can't do in its day-to-day operations. It ends when that work is handed back as something the organization can actually run. In between, it sits on the boundary between two systems that speak different languages and run on different rules.

The organization side

Strategy, funding, authority, governance. It decides which ideas get money, who is accountable, how priorities rank, and how the organization absorbs the result.

The operations side

Capacity, execution, schedules, daily throughput. It owns the staff, the equipment, and the queue of ongoing work, and it inherits whatever the project builds.

You arrive from one side of this line. Organizational management studied the left column; operations management studied the right. This is one of the places where your two courses meet, and where a project that uses both will see more than either alone.

A project gets its mandate and its money from the organization, and borrows its capacity from operations. Until recently, capacity meant people: their hours, their skills, their attention. A project succeeds or fails on how well that trade is negotiated.

The new question: what if the capacity isn't people?

Much of the excitement about agentic AI, software that carries out multistep tasks with some independence, comes from a simple recognition: some work now done by people can be allocated to something that isn't a person. Drafting, scheduling, triaging cases, reconciling records. That changes both sides of the boundary at once.

SideWhat gets harder
OperationsCapacity is no longer only staff hours. Agents don't take overtime, but they cost money to run, fail in unfamiliar ways, and need people to check their work. Remember Mollick's jagged frontier from Online 1: the same agent can be excellent on one task and quietly wrong on the next one that looks just like it, so the checking can't be skipped. Checking becomes its own queue: if agents start work faster than people can review it, the review queue grows, and by Little's Law so does the average wait.
OrganizationThe lines of authority blur. An agent isn't on the org chart. It can't be accountable, sponsor a project, or own a result when the project ends; its decisions trace back to whoever set it up. Is it a cost of labor or of software? Who answers when it gets something wrong?
The dataAgents do work and write records, and records written by an agent describe what the agent did, not necessarily what happened. When the model underneath is updated, the same agent can behave differently: redefined data in a new form.

A Riverbend thought experiment. Suppose Riverbend handed visit documentation to an AI agent. Overtime might fall because the paperwork moved to the agent, not because anyone's workload changed. documentation_errors might fall too, but the agent now writes the very records that quality review checks. And staff_on_roster wouldn't count the agent at all. What would you need to measure to know whether it worked?

You are already inside this question. All module, you have borrowed analytical capacity from an AI analyst while keeping the authority and the accountability yourself: check it, and own the thinking. No one has settled how organizations should do this at scale. If your project touches it, say plainly what your model assumes about who, or what, does the work.

Three interfaces, three data questions

Any project has to settle three things with its parent organization. Each one leaves a mark on the data.

ResourcesWhere do the people and the budget come from?Whose regular work suffered while the project ran?Staff learning a new scheduling system still had visits to make. Look at overtime during the rollout.

How organizations set projects up

Where a project sits in the organization chart shapes who controls it, and also where its records live.

SetupWhere the work sitsWhat it means for the data
FunctionalInside an ordinary department. The line manager keeps control of people and priorities.The data stays in that department's systems, defined the department's way.
MatrixShared. People report to both a project manager and a department head.Two bosses can mean two records and two definitions of the same thing, like two divisions computing turnover differently.
ProjectizedA dedicated team, pulled out of its departments. High focus, and a homecoming that can be hard when the project ends.Project records often leave with the team. The "after" data may have no owner.

Over all of this sits culture: the unwritten habits that decide how fast decisions get made, and whether a project is seen as a step forward or an unwelcome distraction. Culture shows up in data too, in who fills out the survey, who logs the extra hours, and who quietly works around the new system.

The operations side: capacity

On the operations side, a project is a capacity problem. Larson and his coauthors distinguish two kinds:

Time-constrained

The deadline is fixed. If the work falls behind, more people or equipment are added to meet it.

Resource-constrained

The people and equipment available can't be exceeded. If they aren't enough, the schedule slips.

Estimates are made by people with something at stake. A department head asked how long a task will take may pad the answer to protect the department's regular work. That is mismeasured data: what is recorded is a negotiation, not a forecast.

Running work and changing work share one capacity. The hours spent running today's operations plus the hours spent changing them can't add up to more than the hours there are. Start too many projects at once and something has to give.

Little's Law, in words

In any stable system where work arrives, waits, and leaves, the average amount of work in progress equals the rate at which work arrives times the average time each piece spends in the system. Turn it around: the average time in the system equals the work in progress divided by the rate at which work gets finished. That rate can't rise above capacity. So when more work is started than can be finished, work in progress grows, and so does the time every piece takes. It holds for projects in a portfolio and for clients in a queue.

At Riverbend, cases_opened is an arrival rate, median_intake_wait_days is close to a time in the system (a median, where Little's Law uses an average), and staff_on_roster is a rough measure of capacity. If referrals rise and staffing doesn't, waits should rise too: a prediction you could check against the data.

Start with the end

Closing a project is more than delivering a final report. Real closure means confirming the scope was met, training the people who will run the result, handing over the documentation, returning borrowed staff, and recording what was learned. The way to make that go smoothly is to settle two things at the start: who will run the result on its first day, and what objective criteria will count as success.

Acceptance criteria agreed in advance are a measurement decided before the results are in. That is the same rule as honest simulation: say what would count as success before you look, and don't move the line afterward.

Where this fits in your MBA

A project is where the models of the MBA have to work together. Strategy says which projects are worth doing. Accounting supplies the budget. Organizational behavior explains leadership and the pull of two bosses in a matrix. Operations explains capacity and queues. See Models and the MBA. Managed well, a project isn't a disruption to the organization's routine. It is how the organization changes its routine, and that change is what your data has to catch.

The loop closes, almost always incompletely

Evaluation closes a loop. A model of the organization justified the project; the project changed something and produced data; the analysis carries what the data shows back to the model. That loop almost never closes completely. Some of what the project changed was never recorded. Some of what was recorded measures the project, not the outcome. And the people who could tell you what happened have moved on to the next one.

This is Box's point in the reading next to this one: all models are wrong, so the scientist "must be alert to what is importantly wrong." An incomplete loop is the normal condition, not a failure. The job is to say which gaps are tigers and which are mice, and to say it plainly in your white paper.
